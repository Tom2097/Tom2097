India's **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** (the DPDP Act) is now being put into force through the **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025**, which the government notified on 13 November 2025. If your business collects personal data from customers, employees, or leads in India, the Act very likely applies to you.

This checklist sums up the main obligations in plain language. It is not legal advice. Check the official text of the Act and the Rules, and talk to a lawyer for your specific situation.

## When it applies

The Rules come into force in phases rather than all at once:

- **November 2025:** the provisions that set up the Data Protection Board took effect straight away.
- **November 2026:** the provisions on registered consent managers come into force about 12 months after notification.
- **May 2027:** most obligations on businesses apply about 18 months after notification, including notice, consent, security safeguards, breach reporting, and data principal rights.

That gives most businesses until mid-2027, but the work below takes time, so start early.

## Key terms

- **Data Principal:** the person the data is about (your customer, employee, or lead).
- **Data Fiduciary:** the business that decides why and how the data is processed. That is usually you.
- **Data Processor:** a vendor that processes data on your behalf, such as a cloud or software provider.

## The checklist

### 1. Know what personal data you hold

- List every place you collect personal data: website forms, CRM, HR systems, spreadsheets, support inboxes.
- For each, note what you collect, why, who can see it, which vendors process it, and how long you keep it.

### 2. Give a clear notice

- Before or when you ask for consent, tell people what data you collect and why, how they can withdraw consent, how to exercise their rights, and how to complain to the Data Protection Board.
- Write it in clear, plain language. The notice must be available in English or any language listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution, such as Hindi or Punjabi.

### 3. Get valid consent, or rely on a legitimate use

- Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous, given by a clear affirmative action. Pre-ticked boxes do not count.
- Ask only for the data you need for the stated purpose.
- Make withdrawing consent as easy as giving it.
- Keep a record of who consented, to what, and when.
- The Act also lists certain "legitimate uses" where consent is not needed, such as some employment purposes. Check carefully whether one really applies.

### 4. Protect the data

- Put reasonable security safeguards in place: encryption, access controls, logging, backups, and vendor contracts that require the same.
- Failing to take reasonable security safeguards to prevent a breach can bring a penalty of up to ₹250 crore.

### 5. Plan for breaches

- If a personal data breach happens, you must inform the Data Protection Board and each affected person.
- The Rules set out what the notice must contain and require a more detailed follow-up report to the Board. Check the Rules for the exact deadlines.
- Write an incident-response plan now and decide who does what.

### 6. Handle data principal rights

People can ask you to:

- give a summary of the personal data you hold about them and how you process it,
- correct, complete, update, or erase their data,
- resolve their grievances, and
- nominate someone to exercise their rights if they die or become incapacitated.

Publish how people can make these requests, respond within the time the Rules allow, and keep a record of each request.

### 7. Delete data you no longer need

- Erase personal data once the purpose is served or consent is withdrawn, unless a law requires you to keep it.
- Make sure your vendors erase it too.

### 8. Take extra care with children's data

- Get verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian before processing data of anyone under 18.
- Do not track, behaviourally monitor, or target advertising at children.

### 9. Check whether you are a Significant Data Fiduciary

The government can designate some businesses as Significant Data Fiduciaries, based on factors such as the volume and sensitivity of the data they process. These have extra duties, including appointing a Data Protection Officer based in India, running periodic data protection impact assessments, and getting independent audits.
